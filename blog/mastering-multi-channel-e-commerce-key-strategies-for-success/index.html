How to run multi-channel e-commerce: five things that work

When products, inventory, pricing, and orders are scattered across channels, which one to fix first.

EzilyChannels · Ops

On this page

    One back office for Momo. Another for Shopee. A third for your own site. The same product copy has been edited three times, every channel counts its own stock, and last month's price change lives in somebody's memory.

    Listing on more channels is the easy part. The hard part is the hour a day you spend afterward reconciling them.

    These five moves have an order. Working through them in sequence beats attacking all of them at once.

    1. Collapse product data into one master record

    Every channel has its own field rules. Title length caps, image dimensions, how variant options get entered, whether outbound links are allowed. Four channels, four rulebooks. Most teams respond by maintaining a separate copy per channel, and the same SKU ends up describing itself three different ways. Edit one, forget the other two.

    Pull the product into a single master record first: name, specs, selling points, images, materials or ingredients, one version of each. Each channel's required format is generated from that record instead of being rewritten from scratch.

    This goes first because the other four moves all assume one product is one product everywhere. Without the master record, inventory and pricing have nothing to reconcile against.

    What varies by channel is presentation, not substance. Shopee titles carry search terms, Momo spec tables have to be filled out, your own product page can run long. Same facts, different outfits.

    2. Keep one stock number

    Overselling is usually not a demand problem. It happens because each channel quietly reserves its own safety buffer, and the buffers add up to more units than the warehouse is holding.

    One stock number, shared. A sale on any channel reduces what every other channel can sell.

    There is a trade-off: your best sellers will show as out of stock more often. Take it. A stockout is cheaper to recover from than a canceled order, and cancellation costs more than the revenue. It costs support time, a refund cycle, a channel rating, and the next purchase that customer was going to make.

    3. Set base price and channel differences in the same place

    Price should differ between channels. Commission rates differ, shipping economics differ, promotional calendars differ. Selling one product at one price on four channels hands your margin to whichever channel takes the biggest cut.

    The problem is not the spread. The problem is the spread living in four separate back offices. A single price change becomes four edits, one of which gets missed, and a customer usually finds it before you do.

    Put the base price and the channel adjustments in one place. One base price, with each channel's markup, discount, and campaign rules hanging off it. Change the base and the differences follow.

    4. Bring every order into one list

    The slowest part of customer service is not answering the question. It is finding the record. Someone asks whether last week's order shipped, and support has to establish where they bought it before opening the right system to look.

    Put every channel's orders in one list and the lookup becomes a single action. Do the same with returns. One process, not four sets of return rules that happen to match four channel policies, because the customer remembers buying from you, not from a marketplace.

    5. Turn a marketplace order into a person

    The first four moves clean up operations. This one decides your ad budget next quarter.

    When a marketplace sells a unit, you see the order and not the buyer. Name, phone, and address stay on their side. Reaching that same person again means paying for the traffic a second time.

    What you can do is leave an exit. A card in the box. A link in the shipping notification. Warranty records kept in your own member system. Give that person a reason to walk into somewhere that recognizes them. Not everyone will. The ones who do have names from that moment on.

    This step is the slowest, and the hardest to backfill. Doing the first four six months late costs you time. Doing this one six months late means every order in between stayed just an order.

    Automate after the cleanup

    Syncing stock, refreshing listings, pulling orders, routing support tickets: all of it can be handed to software. But automation runs your existing process faster. It will not decide what belongs in the master record, and it will not settle your channel pricing rules for you.

    Automating an unresolved process just produces the same mess at higher speed. Clean up first, then automate.

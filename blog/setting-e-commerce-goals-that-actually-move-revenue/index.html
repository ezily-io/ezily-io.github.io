Set a goal that actually moves revenue

Specific goals work. How to break one into numbers you can see every week.

The revenue target went on slide three in January. Nobody opened that deck after March.

The team did not stop caring. The number just had nothing attached to it. Budgets get adjusted, listings get scheduled, tickets get answered, packages go out, and not one of those tasks visibly moves an annual figure.

Goals usually fail because nobody broke them apart, not because somebody set them too high.

Why a specific goal beats "do your best"

Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory is the foundational work here: specific and difficult goals produce better performance than open-ended instructions to try hard. The reason is not mysterious. A specific goal tells you what to do, how much of it, and when to stop. "Do your best" answers none of the three.

The second finding with research behind it is about tracking itself. Meta-analytic work on progress monitoring finds that the act of checking your own progress raises attainment, and that the effect is stronger when the progress is visible to other people.

So the formula is short: a specific target, plus a standing review. The hard part is not believing it. The hard part is turning it into one sheet you will actually open on a Wednesday.

Break the revenue number into things that move weekly

One rule governs the breakdown. Go down until the number changes week to week, and until you can name who changed it.

Revenue is orders times average order value.

Orders is traffic times conversion rate. Average order value is unit price plus add-ons.

Stop there. Each of those four has an owner and a set of available actions:

  • Traffic: ad budget, social posts, LINE broadcasts, marketplace visibility
  • Conversion rate: product page, checkout flow, free-shipping threshold
  • Unit price: pricing and bundling
  • Add-ons: cart recommendations and spend thresholds

An annual target divided by fifty-two produces a decimal nobody can act on. "Conversion rate goes up this quarter, this person owns it, we look at it every Wednesday" is a sentence somebody can execute.

Push one number per quarter

All four matter. That does not mean you push all four at once. Pushing four at once usually moves none of them, and at quarter end you cannot tell which effort worked.

Pick one. Choose by asking which number is holding the others back right now. Plenty of traffic and weak conversion makes it a product-page and checkout quarter. Healthy conversion and a thin basket makes it a bundling and add-on quarter.

Keep recording the other three. Just do not touch them this quarter. That restraint is what lets you answer "what did we do, and what happened" in December.

Fifteen minutes a week, on the same sheet

The weekly review does not need to be long. One sheet, fixed columns: target, actual, gap, cause, next week's action.

Three rules keep that sheet alive:

Do not change the columns. Conversion rate this week and impressions next week destroys the comparison. The value of a fixed sheet is continuity.

Pull the numbers from the system. Pasting figures by hand survives about three weeks. Then people start skipping it, and the meeting turns into an argument about whether the numbers are right instead of a discussion about why they moved.

Every miss gets a name and an action. In any cell that came in under target, write one person and one thing to do next week. Without those two columns the sheet is a record, not management.

When to change the target

Treating a goal as unchangeable is its own way of failing.

Two situations justify a change. Beating the target substantially two weeks running means the baseline was set too low, so raise it before the team coasts. And if external conditions genuinely shifted, whether that is the market, a marketplace rule change, or supply, the target should move with them. Write down why in the meeting notes, or next quarter nobody will remember where the number came from.

Exactly one situation does not justify it: the deadline is close, the target looks unreachable, so the number quietly comes down. That is not adjusting a goal. That is canceling one.

Where most e-commerce breakdowns stall: repeat purchase

The breakdown above stopped at orders. Orders splits one more time, into first purchases and repeat purchases.

Most teams cannot make that split, because repeat purchase is the number they cannot see. A marketplace order arrives as an order number. Your own store's orders carry an email, but one person checking out twice with two different emails becomes two new customers. When the denominator is uncertain, repeat rate is an estimate wearing a decimal point.

If your breakdown stalls halfway, the problem is usually not the method. It is that customer records were never joined across channels.
